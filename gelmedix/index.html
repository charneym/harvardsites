Restoring vision lost to geographic atrophy.

GelMEDIX delivers lab-grown retinal cells inside an injectable hydrogel that protects them, holds them in place and then dissolves as new tissue takes over.

The problem

Geographic atrophy leaves gaps in the retina's support layer.

The retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) is a single layer of pigmented cells that keeps photoreceptors alive. In late-stage geographic atrophy, patches of it die and vision fades.

47%of people with GA have severe vision loss
Delivery

New cells arrive inside a protective liquid gel.

GMX-101 combines iPSC-derived RPE cells with GelMEDIX's hydrogel. It is injected as a liquid into the space beneath the retina, and the gel shields the cells on the way in.

Engraftment

Light sets the gel, holding the cells where they are needed.

Once in place, the liquid is light-activated into a soft, biodegradable scaffold. It keeps the cells at the target site while they settle, to support cell survival and engraftment.

Result

The scaffold dissolves and new tissue remains.

The hydrogel is designed to degrade naturally as the body's own tissue replaces it. In preclinical studies, GelMEDIX reports improved cell survival, engraftment and vision preservation.

Geographic atrophy slowly erases the layer of cells that keeps the retina alive. Cell therapy could replace them, if the cells survive the injection and stay where they are placed. GelMEDIX builds the hydrogel that makes that possible, and then steps aside.

How it works

Protect, hold, then step aside

Cells for the eye face two problems: surviving the injection, then staying where they are placed. GelMEDIX designs its hydrogel to solve both, and then to degrade as new tissue takes over.

01

Start with RPE cells in a hydrogel

GMX-101 uses retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells derived from induced pluripotent stem cells, made with Catalent's off-the-shelf GMP iPSC lines. They are mixed into a hydrogel made from chemically modified natural polymers.

02

Inject as a liquid

The gel and cells are delivered into the subretinal space, beneath the retina. On the way in, the gel protects the cells during injection.

03

Set it with light

Once in place, the liquid is light-activated into a solid, biodegradable scaffold. It holds the cells at the target site and gives them a structure to settle into, aiming for better cell survival and engraftment.

04

Degrade naturally

Over time the hydrogel breaks down as the body's new tissue replaces it, leaving the cells, not the scaffold.
