Treating hydrocephalus through a vein, not open surgery

The eShunt System is a 3 cm implant, placed by catheter from the groin, that drains excess brain fluid into the bloodstream. It is designed as an alternative to a surgically placed shunt.

Investigational device. Limited by United States law to investigational use.

See how it works →Clinical evidence
The implant

Three parts in about 3 cm

A malecot-style tip anchors in the fluid space. A slender body runs inside the vein, held by a nitinol shroud while the physician adjusts its depth. A one-way slit valve sits at the other end.

Where it sits

Across the wall of a vein beside the brainstem

The implant crosses the dura from the inferior petrosal sinus into the cerebellopontine angle cistern, a pocket of cerebrospinal fluid. Its valve end rests in the internal jugular vein.

Illustrative cutaway, not to scale.

How it drains

Fluid flows one way, into the blood

Like the body's own arachnoid granulations, the implant lets excess cerebrospinal fluid pass through its valve into the venous blood. It is designed to drain 10 mL per hour at a pressure gradient of 8 mm Hg or less.

Placed in about an hour, with a short hospital stay

In a 30-patient US pilot study in normal pressure hydrocephalus, there were no device- or procedure-related serious adverse events.

For more than 60 years, hydrocephalus has been treated with a shunt placed in open surgery through the skull. The eShunt System is designed to do the same job from inside a vein, through a small puncture at the groin.

Why it matters

A common condition, mostly untreated

Normal pressure hydrocephalus is a build-up of cerebrospinal fluid that can cause trouble walking, memory loss and bladder control problems in older adults. The standard treatment is a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt, placed in open surgery.

How it works

From the groin to the brain's fluid space, through the veins

An interventionalist places the implant in an angiography suite under X-ray guidance, in a procedure the company expects to take about an hour. No opening in the skull is needed.

01

Access

The physician enters the femoral vein at the groin through a small puncture.

02

Navigate

A delivery catheter is advanced over a temporary anchor wire up to the jugular vein and into the inferior petrosal sinus, a vein beside the brainstem.

03

Cross the dura

A needle at the catheter tip passes through the wall of the sinus into the cerebellopontine angle cistern, which holds cerebrospinal fluid.

04

Deploy

The implant is released with its tip in the cistern, its body in the sinus and its valve in the internal jugular vein. A nitinol shroud allows depth adjustment before release.

05

Drain

The implant mimics the arachnoid granulations, passively draining excess fluid into the venous system and avoiding the over-drainage seen with conventional shunts.
