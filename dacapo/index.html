02 · A trial in a dish

Aged human neurons,many patients at once.

Neurons made from human iPS cells are modeled in an aged state, where disease phenotypes such as α‑synuclein inclusions can emerge. A Michael J. Fox Foundation–funded program pools up to 100 iPSC lines from PPMI participants to compare fast and slow progressors.

Some lines hold their branches and some lose them. Susceptibility differs from patient to patient.

03 · Network biology

Network biologyfinds the target.

Progression analytics draw insight from small, sparse human datasets. Network biology maps those signals across gene and protein networks to prioritize the drivers. DaCapo reports a novel target, present in an estimated 20–30% of people with Parkinson's.

The unmet need

Medicines for progression, not just risk.Treatments for the many people whoseParkinson's has no inherited cause.

The largest unmet need in neurodegeneration is a medicine that treats disease progression, especially in sporadic disease. Most approaches address risk factors after symptoms appear, or target only rare inherited forms.

To reach progression, new targets have to be found and drugged. Parkinson's is heterogeneous, so DaCapo's approach is built to identify patient subgroups and match treatments to them.

Targeting sporadic Parkinson's

The first known drug target linkedspecifically to progression.

The target connects to α‑synuclein biology and a disease‑associated pathway. When that pathway is disrupted, it can lead to protein misfolding, synaptic dysfunction and neurodegeneration. Sporadic Parkinson's is the most common form of the disease and arises without a defined inherited mutation.

The target may also serve as a biomarker to stratify patients, so that clinical trials enroll the people predicted to respond. That makes trials more precise and more likely to succeed. The work is supported in part by a grant from The Michael J. Fox Foundation for Parkinson's Research.
